Prompt Engineering Case Study

Prompt Design Output Comparison Medical Content

Objective: Test how different prompt structures affect output quality when generating patient education content about diabetes management.

Why This Matters

In AI training work, understanding how models respond to different instruction formats is critical for writing effective annotation guidelines, identifying when outputs fail to follow instructions, and assessing whether a response genuinely addresses the user's need.

Methodology

Tested four prompt approaches with GPT-4 and Claude 3.5 Sonnet:

  • Zero-shot (minimal instruction)
  • Few-shot (with examples)
  • Chain-of-thought (explicit reasoning)
  • Role + constraints (persona + specific requirements)

Task: Generate patient education content explaining blood glucose monitoring for newly diagnosed Type 2 diabetes patients.

Test 1: Zero-Shot Prompt

Prompt

Explain blood glucose monitoring for Type 2 diabetes patients.

GPT-4 Output

Blood glucose monitoring is an essential part of managing Type 2 diabetes. It involves 
regularly checking your blood sugar levels using a glucose meter. Here's what you need 
to know:

1. Why Monitor: Helps track how food, exercise, and medication affect your blood sugar
2. When to Test: Typically before meals and at bedtime, but your doctor will advise
3. Target Ranges: Generally 80-130 mg/dL before meals, less than 180 mg/dL after meals
4. How to Test: Wash hands, insert test strip, prick finger, apply blood to strip
5. Record Results: Keep a log to share with your healthcare provider

Evaluation

  • ✅ Factually accurate
  • ✅ Well-structured
  • ❌ Too clinical for newly diagnosed patients
  • ❌ Assumes knowledge (what's mg/dL?)
  • ❌ Doesn't address common fears
  • ❌ No personalization or reassurance

Quality Score: 6/10 — Correct but not genuinely helpful for the target audience.

Test 2: Few-Shot Prompt

Prompt

Write patient education content. Here are examples of the tone and approach:

Example 1 - Medication adherence:
"Taking your medication every day might feel like a chore at first. That's normal. 
Many people find it helps to link it to something they already do daily — like 
brushing teeth or having morning coffee."

Example 2 - Diet changes:
"You don't have to eat 'diabetes food.' You're eating the same food as everyone else, 
just paying more attention to portions and timing."

Now write similar content explaining blood glucose monitoring.

GPT-4 Output

Checking your blood sugar might seem daunting at first. Most people worry about the 
finger prick. Here's the truth: it's a tiny pinch, less than a mosquito bite. After 
a few days, you won't think about it.

You're not checking because you did something wrong. You're checking to see how your 
body responds to food, stress, and activity. Think of it as feedback, not a test you 
can fail.

Your doctor will tell you when to check — usually before meals and before bed. Write 
the numbers down somewhere you won't lose them. Your phone's notes app works fine.

The meter will show a number. That number isn't good or bad on its own. It's information. 
If it's higher than expected, you're not in trouble. You're just learning what affects 
your levels.

Evaluation

  • ✅ Addresses patient anxiety directly
  • ✅ Reassuring tone without being condescending
  • ✅ Practical advice (phone notes app)
  • ✅ Reframes monitoring as feedback, not judgment
  • ✅ Acknowledges learning curve
  • ⚠️ Lacks specific target ranges

Quality Score: 9/10 — Much better. Sounds like a person talking, not a pamphlet.

Key Findings

What Worked

  • Few-shot examples dramatically improved tone matching
  • Role + constraints produced the tightest, most useful output
  • Chain-of-thought revealed model reasoning (valuable for annotation work)

What Failed

  • Zero-shot generated generic content that looked right but wasn't helpful
  • Outputs without audience specification defaulted to inappropriate clinical tone

Failure Modes Observed

  • Confidence without appropriateness: Zero-shot output was factually correct but wrong for the audience
  • Jargon assumption: Models defaulted to medical terminology without checking appropriateness
  • Missing emotional dimension: Without explicit prompting, outputs ignored patient anxiety

Application to AI Training Work

  • Pairwise comparison: Few-shot vs. zero-shot outputs show clear quality difference despite both being "correct"
  • Instruction-following assessment: Role + constraints test shows whether model can follow specific requirements
  • Annotation criteria: Need to evaluate outputs on multiple dimensions — accuracy alone isn't sufficient
